My refrigerator stopped cooling — what do I do?

A fridge that's running but warm is stressful — you're watching food spoil. Before you assume the worst (an expensive compressor), know this: the large majority of 'not cooling' calls we get turn out to be an affordable fix — airflow, a fan, or the defrost system. Below are the causes in the order we check them, the actual tests (with readings), the parts and honest prices, and the safe checks you can do now.

⚠ Safety first: Unplug the fridge before cleaning coils or testing fans. Sealed-system and compressor work requires a licensed tech and EPA-certified refrigerant handling — never attempt it yourself.

How a refrigerator cools (30 seconds)

The compressor pumps refrigerant through the condenser coils (which dump heat — that's why they need airflow), then through the evaporator coils inside, where an evaporator fan blows the cold air into the compartments. A defrost system periodically melts frost off the evaporator so air keeps flowing. Running-but-warm is almost always airflow, a fan, or defrost — not the compressor.
What we see most: on 'freezer is cold but fridge is warm' the culprit is almost always the evaporator fan or a defrost failure (frost blocking the coil). On 'the whole box is warm and the fridge runs constantly,' start with dirty condenser coils and the condenser fan. A truly dead compressor is the rare exception, not the rule.

The most likely causes — in the order we check them

Dirty condenser coils Easy DIY check

Coils packed with dust and pet hair can't release heat, so the fridge runs nonstop and still won't cool. The most common and most preventable cause.

Test it yourself: Unplug the fridge. Find the coils (behind the bottom kick-plate or on the back) and vacuum/brush them clean. If they were caked, this alone may fix it — give it a few hours to recover.

🔩 Likely part: no part — just cleaning — $0

Tools: Vacuum + coil brushTime: 15–30 min

Evaporator fan motor Technician

This fan moves cold air from the freezer coils into the fridge compartment. If it fails, the freezer may stay somewhat cold while the fridge section warms up.

How a tech tests it: Open the freezer and press the door switch by hand — you should hear the evaporator fan running. Silence points here. With power off, a good motor reads continuity across its windings; open = replace. (A fan blocked by ice points to defrost — see below.)

🔩 Likely part: evaporator fan motor — $30–90

Tools: Multimeter, Nut driverTime: 45–90 min (tech)

Defrost system failure (heater / thermostat / control) Technician

Frost builds on the evaporator and blocks airflow when the defrost heater, defrost thermostat, or timer/board fails. Classic sign: freezer works, fridge doesn't, and you see heavy frost on the back freezer wall.

How a tech tests it: Heavy frost on the back freezer wall is the tell. With power off, a technician tests the defrost heater for continuity (a good heater reads roughly 10–50 ohms; open = bad) and checks the defrost thermostat and control. Manually forcing a defrost that then cools normally confirms it.

🔩 Likely part: defrost heater / thermostat / control — $20–70

Tools: MultimeterTime: 1–2 hrs (tech)

Condenser fan motor Technician

On coil-in-the-back-cabinet models, this fan cools the compressor and condenser. If it stops, the system overheats and cooling drops.

How a tech tests it: With the fridge running you should hear/feel this fan (near the compressor at the back-bottom). Not a safe DIY test — a tech confirms with a continuity check and by watching it run.

🔩 Likely part: condenser fan motor — $40–90

Tools: MultimeterTime: 45–90 min (tech)

Start relay / compressor Technician

The compressor is the heart of the system. A failed start relay (cheap) can stop it from starting; a failed compressor (expensive) is the rare worst case.

How a tech tests it: A tech pulls the start relay and shake-tests it (a rattle = burnt), and checks continuity on the compressor's three pins. This isn't a DIY test — it confirms whether it's the cheap relay or the compressor itself.

🔩 Likely part: start relay (cheap) — or compressor (rare) — relay $10–30 · compressor ~$250–500 installed

Tools: MultimeterTime: 1 hr (relay) / half-day (compressor)

Blocked vents or an overpacked fridge Easy DIY check

Packing food against the interior air vents stops cold air from circulating between compartments.

Test it yourself: Make sure nothing is jammed against the vents inside the fridge and freezer (usually back wall / between compartments). Leave a gap and give it a few hours.

🔩 Likely part: no part — $0

Time: 5 min

Tools you'll need for the DIY checks

  • Multimeter (fan + defrost heater tests)
  • Vacuum + coil brush
  • Nut driver / screwdriver set
  • A fridge thermometer to confirm recovery

Repair or replace? (the honest math)

Coils, fans, start relays, and defrost parts are all inexpensive — typically $0–$90 in parts — and worth repairing. Common questions

Why is my refrigerator running but not cooling?

Running-but-warm is almost always airflow or a fan, not the compressor. Most common: dirty condenser coils, a failed evaporator fan, or a defrost failure that frosts over the coil. Start by vacuuming the coils.

My freezer is cold but the fridge is warm — why?

That specific pattern usually means the evaporator fan isn't moving cold air into the fridge, or the defrost system failed and frost is blocking the coil. Look for heavy frost on the back freezer wall.

How do I know if it’s the compressor?

Rarely is it. A tech shake-tests the start relay (a cheap part that often mimics a dead compressor) and checks the compressor pins before condemning it. Don't assume compressor from 'not cooling' alone.

Can I fix a fridge that’s not cooling myself?

The safe DIY steps: vacuum the condenser coils, clear blocked interior vents, and listen for the evaporator fan. Fan, defrost, relay, and any sealed-system work are technician repairs.

Is a refrigerator that won’t cool worth fixing?

Usually yes — most causes are cheap parts. The exception is a bad compressor or refrigerant leak; for those we give you honest repair-vs-replace numbers first.
