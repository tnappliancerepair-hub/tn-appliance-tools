My oven won't heat — what do I do?

When the oven won't come up to temperature, dinner's on hold. Most of the time it traces to one affordable part — the bake element on electric ovens, the igniter on gas. Below are the causes in the order we check them, the actual tests (with readings), the parts and honest prices, and what you can safely check yourself.

⚠ Safety first: Cut power to the oven at the breaker before any internal check. Gas oven + gas smell → stop, ventilate, call a pro. Bake elements carry line voltage — only touch them with the power off.

How an oven heats (30 seconds)

An electric oven heats with a bake element (bottom) and a broil element (top), controlled by a board using a temperature sensor to hold the setpoint. A gas oven uses an igniter that glows hot enough to open the gas safety valve and light the burner. Electric ranges run on 240V, so losing half the circuit can leave the cooktop working but the oven cold.
What we see most: on electric ovens, a burned-out bake element (often with a visible break or blister) is the everyday cause; on gas ovens it's almost always a weak igniter — it still glows, which fools people, but it's too weak to open the gas valve.

The most likely causes — in the order we check them

Burned-out bake element (electric) Moderate DIY

The element cracks, blisters, or breaks and stops glowing evenly — so the oven can't heat.

Test it yourself: With the oven on, watch the bottom bake element — it should glow a uniform bright orange. Dark spots or no glow = replace it. Confirm with power off: a good element reads roughly 15–40 ohms; an open reading = burned out.

🔩 Likely part: bake element — $25–60

Tools: Multimeter, Nut driverTime: 20–40 min

Weak igniter (gas) Technician

The most common gas-oven failure. The igniter weakens with age; it still glows but can't draw enough current to open the gas valve — so no flame, no heat.

How a tech tests it: Watch the igniter when you call for heat: if it glows but the burner never lights, it's almost always the igniter. A tech confirms with an amp-draw test (a healthy igniter pulls enough current to open the valve; a weak one doesn't).

🔩 Likely part: oven igniter — $30–60

Tools: Multimeter (amp clamp)Time: 45–90 min (tech)

Temperature sensor (oven probe) Moderate DIY

If the sensor reads wrong, the control won't apply heat or holds the wrong temperature.

How a tech tests it: With power off, a good sensor reads about 1,080–1,100 ohms at room temperature and rises as it heats. A wildly off or open reading = replace. (If the oven's off by a lot but the element looks fine, suspect the sensor.)

🔩 Likely part: oven temperature sensor — $15–40

Tools: MultimeterTime: 30–45 min

Half-power to an electric range (240V) Easy DIY check

Electric ranges use 240V. If one leg trips, the cooktop may still work on 120V while the oven stays cold.

Test it yourself: Fully switch the range's double-pole breaker OFF, then back ON. Cooktop works but oven's cold is a classic lost-leg symptom.

🔩 Likely part: no part (breaker reset) — unless breaker faulty — $0

Tools: Voltage tester (optional)Time: 5 min

Control board / electronic control Technician

A failed control board can leave the oven unresponsive or refusing to heat.

How a tech tests it: Diagnosed by a technician after the element/igniter/sensor are ruled out — the pricier part, so we confirm it's actually the board before replacing.

🔩 Likely part: oven control board — $80–250

Tools: MultimeterTime: 1–2 hrs (tech)

Tools you'll need for the DIY checks

  • Multimeter (element, sensor tests; amp clamp for a gas igniter)
  • Nut driver / screwdriver set
  • Voltage tester (optional, for the 240V check)

Common questions

Why won’t my oven heat but the cooktop works?

On an electric range that's a classic lost-leg (240V) symptom — cycle the double-pole breaker. If that doesn't do it, the bake element is the next suspect (look for a visible break or no glow).

How do I test an oven bake element?

Watch it with the oven on — it should glow uniform bright orange. With power off, a multimeter reads roughly 15–40 ohms on a good element; an open reading means it's burned out.

My gas oven igniter glows but won’t light — why?

That's the #1 gas-oven failure: the igniter is too weak to open the gas safety valve even though it still glows. It needs replacing — a tech confirms with an amp-draw test.

Can I fix an oven that won’t heat myself?

The bake element and the 240V breaker check are DIY-friendly with power off. Gas igniters, temperature sensors, and control boards are best left to a technician.

Is an oven that won’t heat worth fixing?

Usually yes — element, igniter, and sensor are cheap. Only the control board is pricey, and we'll give you honest numbers before replacing it.
