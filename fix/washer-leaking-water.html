My washer is leaking water — what do I do?

A leaking washer needs attention fast — laundry-room water damage gets expensive quickly. The good news is most leaks trace to a handful of spots, and several are things you can check or prevent yourself. The first job is figuring out WHERE the water is coming from, because that points straight at the cause.

⚠ Safety first: Unplug the washer and shut off both water valves before checking hoses or connections, and mop up standing water so you don't slip. If the leak is behind the machine, don't reach into a wet outlet area.

The most likely causes

Worn or loose fill hoses Easy DIY check

The rubber inlet hoses at the back crack and blister with age — they're a leading cause of laundry-room floods, especially the ones more than 3–5 years old. A loose connection at the valve or washer also drips.

Check it: Check both hoses for bulges, cracks, or dampness at the fittings and snug the connections. If the rubber is old, replace them (braided stainless hoses last far longer) — this is cheap insurance we handle on almost any visit.

Too much detergent / oversudsing Easy DIY check

Using regular (non-HE) detergent or too much of it in a high-efficiency washer creates excess suds that overflow and look exactly like a leak. It's one of the most common "leaks" that isn't a broken part.

Check it: Switch to HE detergent and use only the recommended small amount. Run a rinse-only cycle to clear existing suds.

Torn door boot / gasket (front-load) Moderate DIY check

The rubber bellows around a front-load door catches coins and hair, then tears or molds, letting water spray out the front during the wash.

Check it: Inspect the folds of the gasket for tears, debris, or a bent spring; wipe it out. A torn boot is a tech replacement.

Loose or clogged drain hose Moderate DIY check

If the drain hose isn't secured in the standpipe, or it clogs and backs up, water can push out during the drain/spin — usually a leak that only shows up at the end of the cycle.

Check it: Make sure the drain hose is pushed in and secured at the standpipe and not kinked. A clog in the pump or hose is a tech job.

Failed tub seal or pump Moderate DIY check

The pump, its hose clamps, or the main tub seal can leak from underneath — you'll see water pooling under the center of the machine rather than at the hoses.

Check it: Water under the middle of the washer points inside — that's a tech diagnosis and repair.

Is it worth fixing?

Almost always repair. Hoses, gaskets, and pump clamps are affordable, and stopping a leak protects your floors and the room below. Replacement only comes up if an old machine has a major tub-seal failure stacked with other problems.

Common questions

Why is my washer leaking from the bottom?

Water pooling under the center usually means an internal leak — the pump, a hose clamp, or the tub seal. Water at the back points at the fill hoses; water at the front of a front-loader points at the door boot. Where it pools tells us the cause.

Should I replace my washer hoses?

Yes — rubber fill hoses should be replaced every 3–5 years because a burst hose is one of the top causes of home water damage. Braided stainless hoses last much longer. We can swap them on almost any service visit.
